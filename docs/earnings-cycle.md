# Earnings-cycle overlay: hold chip stocks only around earnings

A second, separate bot. It trades up to 22 US chip and big-tech stocks with a
small budget (£50 by default), and it never touches the VUAG bot's fund or your
own NVDA.

## The idea in one paragraph

Two well-known effects suggest that a stock tends to do well **just after**
its earnings release and **in the weeks before the next one**:
post-earnings drift (Bernard & Thomas 1989) and the earnings-announcement
premium (Frazzini & Lamont 2007; Barber et al. 2013). So the rule holds each
stock for the first 3 sessions after its earnings filing (counting the
reaction day), and again from session 46 until the next release. In between,
that stock's slot sits in cash. Each of the 23 stocks has a fixed slot of
1/23, and on a typical day about 31% is invested.

| Sessions since earnings | 0-2 | 3-20 | 21-45 | 46+ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Position | **hold** | cash | cash | **hold** |

## How "sessions since earnings" is worked out

1. **Earnings date.** From SEC EDGAR: every Form 8-K that has Item 2.02
   ("Results of Operations"), plus three earnings filings that were tagged
   2.01 by mistake (AMAT 2024-05-16, LRCX 2016-04-20, SWKS 2015-11-05).
2. **Reaction day.** The first NYSE session whose close comes *after* the
   filing's acceptance time. Most companies file at about 16:05 New York time,
   just after the close, so the market reacts the next day. SEC's timestamp
   ends in `Z`, which means UTC. Read it as UTC: reading it as New York time
   moves almost every release into the trading day and breaks the rule.
3. **Count.** NYSE sessions since the most recent reaction day. The reaction
   day itself is 0, and weekends and holidays don't count.

Code: [`src/wikiquant/earnings_cycle.py`](../src/wikiquant/earnings_cycle.py),
with a test for each of these steps in
[`tests/test_earnings_cycle.py`](../tests/test_earnings_cycle.py).

## Timing: why trades are a day late

| When | What happens |
|---|---|
| Evening of day t, after the close | Work out the targets from data up to the close of t, and log them |
| Near the close of day t+1 | Trade to those targets |
| Day t+2 | First return earned |

This one-day gap is deliberate. A filing at 16:05 on day t is already public
that evening, but it still doesn't count for day t's signal, because its
reaction day is t+1. Using it early would be look-ahead bias, and the backtest
would look better than real life.

## Results so far (from the plan, re-checked by `verify`)

| Period | Strategy | Equal-weight buy-and-hold |
|---|---|---|
| 2022-2023, out of sample, 5 bps per side | Sharpe 0.58, +15%, max drawdown -10.5% | Sharpe ~0.33, +11-13%, max drawdown -39% |
| 2017-2021, in sample | Sharpe 1.48, +104% | Sharpe 1.20, +340% |

Our own rebuild is in [`reports/earnings_cycle.md`](../reports/earnings_cycle.md),
regenerated by `python scripts/earnings_bot.py verify` (also run on GitHub on
every change to the strategy code).

**Read this before trusting it:**
- The main benefit is a much smaller drawdown. In a long bull market it earns
  far less than simply holding, because it is mostly in cash.
- 172 rules were tried in the research behind it. After correcting for that
  many tries (the "deflated Sharpe ratio"), it is a promising candidate, not a
  proven edge.
- The sector (chips and big tech) was picked after a decade in which it boomed.

## Running it with real money: what changes at £50

E chose to trade this for real with a £50 budget instead of paper trading.
That's fine, but the numbers are small, and a few things differ from the backtest:

- **Slot size.** £50 / 23 ≈ £2.17 per stock. Trading 212 accepts fractional
  shares, so this works, but orders under £1 are skipped. In practice the bot
  buys when a stock enters its window and sells when it leaves. It doesn't do
  the tiny daily top-ups that the backtest assumes.
- **Currency fee.** A GBP account pays 0.15% to convert to USD on each trade.
  At about 8 times a year of turnover, the plan's cost table puts the Sharpe
  at about 0.52 instead of 0.58. US dividends also lose 15% to withholding
  tax (with a W-8BEN form).
- **NVDA is skipped.** You trade NVDA yourself, so the bot never buys or sells
  it (set in `config/earnings_cycle.json` under `skip`). Its signal is still
  logged, so the log stays comparable with the original research, but the
  bot's real holdings will differ from the logged weights on NVDA days.
- **Fixed budget.** Each slot is always £50/23. Gains aren't reinvested, and
  losses are topped up from the account's free cash when a stock is bought again.
- **Timing risk.** GitHub's scheduler can start late. The bot only trades
  between 60 and 3 minutes before the US close, so a late run skips that day
  instead of trading after hours.

## Safety switches

| You want | What you set (repository Settings > Secrets and variables > Actions) |
|---|---|
| Nightly signal log only | variable `EARNINGS_BOT_ENABLED` = `true`, secret `SEC_USER_AGENT` |
| Dry-run trades in the log | (the above) + secrets `T212_API_KEY` / `T212_API_SECRET` (practice key first) |
| Practice orders actually placed | + variable `EARNINGS_EXECUTE` = `true` |
| Real money | live key + variable `EARNINGS_T212_ENV` = `live` + variable `EARNINGS_CONFIRM_LIVE` = `yes` |

Also built in:
- every single order is capped at £10 (`max_order_gbp`);
- it stops before sending anything if the account holds much more of a stock
  than one slot, because those shares are probably yours;
- it never retries an order (Trading 212 market orders can execute twice);
- it doesn't trade while an earlier order for one of its stocks is pending;
- it trades at most once a day.

The VUAG bot uses the same Trading 212 key. The two bots trade different
instruments, so neither sells the other's holdings, but both spend the same
free cash. Keep enough cash in the account for both.

`SEC_USER_AGENT` is your name and email, for example `Jane Doe jane@example.com`.
SEC requires it from every program that downloads filings. It's a secret, so
it never appears in the public logs or the code.

## The signal log (tamper-evident)

Each evening one line goes into
[`ledger/earnings/signals.jsonl`](../ledger/earnings/signals.jsonl): the
signal date, the number of live names, the share invested, every weight, the
sessions-since-earnings counts, and SHA-256 hashes of the earnings data, the
config and the code. Each line also stores the hash of the line before it:

    hash = SHA-256(previous hash + the line's JSON with sorted keys)

So editing an old line breaks every hash after it. The bot checks the chain
before adding a line and refuses to extend a broken log. This is the same
idea a blockchain uses, at the scale of one text file.

The trades log (`ledger/earnings/trades.jsonl`) records only what was done
(buy, sell, skip), with no amounts, because the repository is public.

**Frozen for 13 weeks.** Don't change the universe, the bucket map, the timing
or the costs. `plan_sha256` in each log line is the hash of
`config/earnings_cycle.json`, so a change shows up. If a bug fix changes a
signal, write down the date and the reason, and report the affected days separately.

## Things to explain in an interview

- Why the reaction day, not the filing date, and why the UTC reading matters.
- Why weights from day t are traded on t+1 and earn from t+2 (look-ahead bias).
- Why it isn't scaled up to 100% invested: the cash is part of the rule, and
  the drawdown benefit comes from it.
- Why one good out-of-sample test isn't proof after 172 tries (multiple testing).
- What the hash chain proves: the signals weren't edited after the fact.
