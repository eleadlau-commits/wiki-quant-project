# The broad test: does attention still work on ~500 stocks, after costs?

Generated by `scripts/broad_study.py` from `data/broad/` · prices 2016-01-04 to 2026-11-10.

**Verdict: Does not survive: after costs and out of sample, it is indistinguishable from luck.**

## What changed from the 16-stock dashboard

- **Universe fixed in advance.** Every company in Wikipedia's *List of S&P 500 companies* as it stood on 2016-01-01 ([that exact version](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?oldid=697200065)): 500 companies. Nobody in 2016 knew which of them would boom, shrink or be bought.
- **Delisted companies kept where free data allows.** 172 of the 500 have since left the index; we have prices for 76 of those. Prices found for 394 companies in total (Yahoo 394, Stooq 0); **106 are missing**, mostly companies that were bought out (Yahoo deletes their history). 0 of the stocks we do have stopped trading during the test.
- **Trading costs.** 10 bps (0.10%) per trade each way, charged on the actual change in weights.
- **Out of sample.** 2016-2020 vs 2021-2026. Settings may only be chosen on the first part.
- **Luck test.** The same strategy with scores shuffled between stocks, 200 times.
- **Month-clustered t-stats** for spike events, because spikes bunch up on market-wide news.

## Limits that remain

- 106 companies have no free price history (mostly buyouts). Buyouts usually come at a premium, so leaving them out probably makes *any* long strategy look slightly worse, not better; but we cannot test it.
- Wikipedia page titles come from links in the 2016 list; a few may point at the wrong page or a parent company. Pages with under 100 views a day are ignored.
- Costs are a flat estimate. Real costs are higher for small, volatile stocks, which is where attention spikes cluster.
- The luck test shuffles stocks within each week, so it keeps the market's ups and downs and only breaks the link between attention and which stocks were picked.
